# Motor effect

## Magnetic fields around currents

### Straight wire

When a current flows through a conducting wire, a magnetic field is produced around it. The shape and direction of this field can be investigated using plotting compasses.

![Diagram of a wire passing through a plane showing concentric magnetic field lines and the direction of current](../assets/images/page_375_image_1_v2.jpg)

*Diagram showing the magnetic field around a current-carrying wire*

The magnetic field is made up of **concentric circles** — a circular field pattern indicates that the magnetic field around a current-carrying wire has **no poles**. As the distance from the wire increases, the circles get further apart, showing that the field is strongest closest to the wire and gets weaker further away.

The **right-hand thumb rule** can be used to work out the **direction** of the magnetic field.

![Illustration of the right-hand thumb rule showing a hand gripping a wire with the thumb pointing up in the direction of current and other fingers curling in the direction of the magnetic field.](../assets/images/page_376_image_2_v2.jpg)

*The right-hand thumb rule shows the direction of current flow through a wire and the direction of the magnetic field around the wire*

Reversing the direction in which the current flows through the wire **reverses the direction** of the magnetic field.

![Diagrams showing side and top views of magnetic field lines around a wire for current flowing up (out of the plane) and down (into the plane).](../assets/images/page_376_image_1_v2.jpg)

*Side and top view of the current flowing through a wire and the magnetic field produced*

If there is **no current** flowing through the conductor there is **no magnetic field**. Increasing the current increases the strength of the magnetic field, shown by the field lines becoming **closer together**. More generally, the strength of the field around a straight wire depends on the size of the current and the distance from the wire: a larger **current** produces a larger **magnetic field** (and vice versa), and the greater the **distance** from the wire, the **weaker** the magnetic field (and vice versa).

![Diagram showing two wires with current flowing through them. The left wire has fewer magnetic field lines, labeled "MAGNETIC FIELD LINES BECOME FURTHER APART -> FIELD BECOMES WEAKER AWAY FROM WIRE". The right wire has more concentrated field lines, labeled "CURRENT INCREASED" and "STRONGER MAGNETIC FIELD (FIELD LINES CLOSE TOGETHER)".](../assets/images/page_377_image_1_v2.jpg)

*The greater the current, the stronger the magnetic field. This is shown by more concentrated field lines*

### Flat circular coil

When a wire is looped into a **coil**, the magnetic field lines circle around each part of the coil, passing through the centre of it.

![Diagram showing the magnetic field around a flat circular coil with circular field lines around the wire and a larger loop through the center.](../assets/images/page_391_chart_1_v2.jpg)

*The magnetic field around a flat circular coil*

To increase the strength of the magnetic field, the wire can be coiled further to form a **solenoid**. The magnetic field around a solenoid is similar to that of a **bar magnet**, so we can draw the pattern of magnetic field lines of a current-carrying solenoid in the same way.

![Diagram showing the magnetic field lines around and through a solenoid, with labels for South Pole and North Pole.](../assets/images/page_392_image_1_v2.jpg)

*Magnetic field around and through a solenoid. This is similar to the field of a bar magnet.*

### Solenoid

The magnetic field inside a solenoid is strong and **uniform**: inside the solenoid, the fields from individual turns **add together** to form a very strong, almost uniform field along its **centre**, while outside the solenoid they **cancel** to give a weaker field. One end of the solenoid behaves like the **north pole** of a magnet, and the other like the **south pole** — to work out which is which, view the solenoid from one end: if the current travels **clockwise**, that end is the **south pole**; if it travels **anticlockwise**, that end is the **north pole**. If the current changes direction, the north and south poles are reversed. If there is no current flowing through the wire, there is no magnetic field produced around or through the solenoid.

![Diagram showing the right-hand rule for a solenoid, with end views indicating N and S poles based on current direction.](../assets/images/page_393_image_1_v2.jpg)

*Poles of a solenoid. The right hand rule can be adapted for this situation, with fingers following the direction of current and the thumb pointing in the direction of the central magnetic field lines.*

#### Factors affecting a solenoid's field strength

The strength of the magnetic field produced around a solenoid can be increased by:

* Increasing the **size of the current** flowing through the wire

* Increasing the **number of turns in the coil** in a given length

* **Reducing the length** of the wire while **maintaining the number of turns**

* Adding an **iron core** through the centre of the coils — the core becomes an **induced magnet** while current flows, making the solenoid's magnetic field much **stronger** overall

!!! tip "Examiner Tips and Tricks"

    Remember the term 'uniform field' means a field which has the same strength and direction at all points. This is represented by parallel field lines.

    When discussing the strength of an electromagnet, avoid saying "add more coils": the coil describes the overall object — the individual loops of wire should be referred to as **turns**. The correct phrase to use is "add more **turns** to the coil".

### Electromagnets

When an electric current flows through a wire, it creates a magnetic field around it; winding the wire into a **coil** concentrates the field lines and strengthens the field further. Winding the coil around a soft magnetic core, such as iron, creates an **electromagnet** (see diagram below) — the electromagnet is magnetic **only** while current flows through the wire.

![Diagram of an electromagnet showing a coil of wire wrapped around a soft iron core with current direction arrows](../assets/images/page_389_chart_1_v2.jpg)

***Electromagnets are made up of a coil of wire wrapped around an iron core***

The magnetic field around an electromagnet has the same shape as the field around a bar magnet, but unlike a bar magnet — which is always magnetic — it can be switched off, or have its field **reversed** by reversing the direction of the current. Its strength can be increased in the same ways as a solenoid's (see above): increasing the current, or adding more turns to the coil.

## The motor effect

### Magnetic force on a current-carrying wire

The **motor effect** occurs when a wire carrying a current is placed in a magnetic field and experiences a **force**. This is the result of two interacting magnetic fields: one produced around the wire by the current flowing through it, and the other the external field it is placed in (for example, between two magnets). Their interaction produces a force on the wire — but if no current flows through the conductor, it experiences no force.

![Diagram showing a wire with current passing through a magnetic field between North and South poles, experiencing a downward force. Labels include CURRENT, FIELD, and FORCE ON THE WIRE.](../assets/images/page_378_image_1_v2.jpg)

*The motor effect is a result of two magnetic fields interacting to produce a force on the wire*

### Factors affecting the magnetic force

Magnetic forces arise from interactions between magnetic fields: **stronger** magnetic fields produce **stronger** forces, and vice versa. For a current-carrying conductor, the force exerted can be increased by increasing the **current** flowing through the wire (which increases the magnetic field around the wire), using **stronger magnets** (which increases the field between the poles), or placing the wire at **90°** to the magnetic field lines between the poles, which gives the maximum interaction between the two fields. If the two magnetic fields are **parallel**, there is no interaction between them, and so **no force** is produced.

### Fleming's left-hand rule

The **direction** of the **force** (or **thrust**) on a current-carrying wire depends on the direction of the current and the magnetic field: all three — force, field and current — are **perpendicular** to each other, so in Fleming's left-hand rule questions the force can sometimes point into or out of the page. The direction of the force can be worked out using **Fleming's left-hand rule**:

![Diagram of Fleming's left-hand rule showing the thumb as Thrust, first finger as Field, and second finger as Current, all perpendicular to each other.](../assets/images/page_386_image_1_v2.jpg)

*Fleming's left-hand rule can be used to determine the directions of the force, magnetic field and current*

!!! example "Worked Example (method)"

    A current-carrying wire is placed into the magnetic field between the poles of the magnet, as shown in the diagram.

    ![Diagram showing a wire carrying current through a magnetic field between North and South poles.](../assets/images/page_387_image_1_v2.jpg)

    Use Fleming's left-hand rule to show that there will be a downward force acting on the wire.

    ??? success "Answer:"

        **Step 1: Determine the direction of the magnetic field**

        Point your **F**irst **F**inger in the direction of the (magnetic) **F**ield.

        **Step 2: Determine the direction of the current**

        Rotate your hand around the first finger so that the se**C**ond finger points in the direction of the **C**urrent.

        **Step 3: Determine the direction of the force**

        The **TH**umb now points in the direction of the **TH**rust (the force) — this is the direction in which the wire will move.

        ![Illustration of Fleming's Left-Hand Rule showing the thumb (Thrust), first finger (Field), and second finger (Current) at right angles.](../assets/images/page_387_image_2_v2.jpg)

!!! tip "Examiner Tips and Tricks"

    Remember that the magnetic field is always in the direction from **North** to **South**.

    Feel free to use your hands when answering Fleming's left hand rule questions, just don't make it too obvious or distracting for other students!

### The D.C. motor

The **motor effect** can be used to create a simple **d.c.** electric motor: the force on a current-carrying coil is used to make it **rotate** in a single direction. The simple D.C. motor consists of a coil of wire (which is free to rotate) positioned in a **uniform magnetic field**. The coil of wire, when horizontal, forms a complete circuit with a cell. The coil is attached to a **split ring** (a circular tube of metal split in two), which is connected in a circuit with the cell via contact with conducting **carbon brushes**.

![Diagram of a D.C. motor showing a coil between N and S magnetic poles, connected to a split ring and carbon brushes, with forces F acting on the horizontal coil.](../assets/images/page_379_image_1_v2.jpg)

***Forces acting in opposite directions on each side of the coil, causing it to rotate. The split ring connects the coil to the flow of current***

Current flowing through the coil produces a magnetic field, which interacts with the uniform external field, so a **force** is exerted on the wire. Forces act in opposite directions on each side of the coil, causing it to rotate: on the blue side, current travels towards the cell, so the force acts upwards (using **Fleming's left-hand rule**); on the black side, current flows away from the cell, so the force acts downwards. Once the coil has rotated 90°, the split ring is **no longer in contact** with the brushes, so no current flows through the coil and no forces act.

![Diagram of a DC motor with a vertical coil, showing magnets (N and S), carbon brushes, split ring commutator, and a cell.](../assets/images/page_380_image_1_v2.jpg)

*No force acts on the coil when vertical, as the split ring is not in contact with the brushes*

Even though no force acts, the momentum of the coil carries it round slightly further, until the split ring reconnects with the carbon brushes and current flows through the coil again — now with the blue side on the right and the black side on the left. Current still flows toward the cell on the left and away from the cell on the right, even though the coil has flipped, so the black side now experiences an upward force on the left and the blue side a downward force on the right: the coil continues to rotate in the same direction, forming a continuously spinning motor.

![Diagram of a D.C. motor showing magnets, coil, split ring, and carbon brushes](../assets/images/page_381_image_1_v2.jpg)

*Even though the coil has flipped, current still flows anticlockwise and the forces still cause rotation in the same direction*

#### Factors affecting the D.C. motor

The **speed** at which the coil rotates can be increased by:

* Increasing the **current**

* Increasing the strength of the **magnetic field**

The **direction of rotation** of the coil can be changed by:

* Reversing the direction of the **current**

* Reversing the direction of the magnetic field, by reversing the **poles** of the magnet

The **force** supplied by the motor can be increased by:

* Increasing the **current** in the coil

* Increasing the strength of the **magnetic field**

* Adding **more turns** to the coil

### Loudspeakers

Loudspeakers and headphones convert electrical signals into sound, working due to the **motor effect** — the opposite way to microphones. A loudspeaker consists of a **coil of wire** wrapped around one pole of a **permanent magnet**.

![Diagram of a loudspeaker cross-section showing magnet, coil, and speaker cone](../assets/images/page_382_image_1_v2.jpg)

*Diagram showing a cross-section of a loudspeaker*

An **alternating current** passes through the coil, creating a **changing magnetic field** around it. As the current is constantly changing direction, this magnetic field is also constantly changing direction. The changing field **interacts** with the field from the permanent magnet, and the interacting fields exert a **force** on the coil — the direction of this force at any instant can be found using **Fleming's left-hand rule**. Because the magnetic field is constantly changing direction, the force on the coil **constantly changes direction** too, making the coil **oscillate**. The oscillating coil causes the speaker cone to oscillate, which makes the air oscillate, creating **sound waves**.

!!! example "Worked Example (method)"

    A d.c. motor is set up as shown below.

    ![Diagram of a simple DC motor with a coil between two magnets (South and North poles) connected to a battery.](../assets/images/page_383_image_1_v2.jpg)

    Determine whether the coil will be rotating clockwise or anticlockwise.

    ??? success "Answer:"

        **Step 1: Draw arrows to show the direction of the magnetic field lines**

        These go from the north pole of the magnet to the south pole of the magnet.

        ![Diagram showing magnetic field lines (blue arrows) pointing from the North pole to the South pole across the coil.](../assets/images/page_383_image_6_v2.jpg)

        **Step 2: Draw arrows to show the direction the current is flowing in the coils**

        Current flows from the positive terminal of the battery to the negative terminal.

        ![Diagram showing current direction (red arrows) in the coil, flowing from the positive terminal to the negative terminal, in addition to the magnetic field lines.](../assets/images/page_383_image_3_v2.jpg)

        **Step 3: Use Fleming's left-hand rule to determine the direction of the force on each side of the coil**

        Point your **F**irst **F**inger in the direction of the (magnetic) **F**ield, rotate your hand around the first finger so the se**C**ond finger points in the direction of the **C**urrent, and the **TH**umb now points in the direction of the **TH**rust (the force).

        ![Diagram showing a coil between two magnets (S and N) with force arrows (F) pointing up on the left side and down on the right side.](../assets/images/page_384_image_1_v2.jpg)

        **Step 4: Use the force arrows to determine the direction of rotation**

        The coil will be turning **clockwise**.

        ![Diagram showing the same motor setup as above, but with an added arrow indicating clockwise rotation.](../assets/images/page_384_image_2_v2.jpg)

!!! tip "Examiner Tips and Tricks"

    It is important to remember all the steps that cause the rotation of the coil in a d.c. motor. Use Fleming's Left Hand rule to convince yourself of the direction of the force on each side of the coil, these should be in opposite directions because the directions of the current through each side are opposite.

    Additionally, don't be confused if you see the phrase 'split-ring commutator'. This is another way of referring to the split ring in the circuit and they mean the same thing.

    The explanation of the loudspeaker is very similar to the explanation of a motor, however **direct current** is used in a d.c motor and **alternating current** is used in a loudspeaker. You need to learn how both work.

    When explaining how a loudspeaker works remember to refer to the **alternating current** and the **changing magnetic field** that it creates.

## Magnetic force on a moving charge

When a current-carrying wire is placed in a magnetic field, it experiences a force if the wire is perpendicular to the field — this is because the field exerts a force on each individual electron flowing through the wire. So when a charged particle passes through a magnetic field, the field can exert a **force** on it, causing it to **deflect**. This force is always at **90 degrees** to both the particle's direction of travel and the magnetic field lines, and its direction can be worked out using **Fleming's left-hand rule**.

![Diagram showing an electron being deflected upwards as it passes through a magnetic field between a North and South pole.](../assets/images/page_395_image_1_v2.jpg)

*The electron experiences a force upwards when it travels through the magnetic field between the two poles. Remember that conventional current flows in the opposite direction to electrons.*

If the particle is travelling **perpendicular** to the field lines, it experiences the **maximum** force. If it is travelling **parallel** to the field lines, it experiences **no** force. If it is travelling at an **angle** to the field lines, it experiences a **smaller** force.

!!! tip "Examiner Tips and Tricks"

    Remember that the direction of current flow in Fleming's Left-Hand Rule is from **positive** to **negative**. This means it is in the **opposite** direction to the direction of travel of an electron (which is negatively charged)
